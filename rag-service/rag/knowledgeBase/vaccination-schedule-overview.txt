Title: Understanding the Infant Vaccination Schedule
Topic: vaccination, immunization

Infant vaccination schedules are designed to protect babies during the age
windows when they're most vulnerable to specific diseases, often requiring
multiple doses spaced weeks apart to build full protection.

Common vaccines in a typical early schedule and what they protect against:
- BCG: protects against severe forms of tuberculosis, often given at birth in
  countries where TB is more common
- Hepatitis B: protects against a virus that can cause serious liver disease;
  often started at birth
- Pentavalent (DTP-HepB-Hib) combination: protects against diphtheria,
  tetanus, pertussis (whooping cough), hepatitis B, and Haemophilus
  influenzae type b
- Polio (OPV/IPV): protects against poliovirus, given in multiple doses
- Rotavirus: protects against a common cause of severe infant diarrhea and
  vomiting
- MMR: protects against measles, mumps, and rubella, typically given from
  around 9-12 months onward, sometimes with a booster dose later

Why doses are spaced out:
Multiple doses of the same vaccine, given weeks apart, are usually needed to
build strong, lasting immunity — a single dose alone often isn't enough. Mild
side effects like a low fever, fussiness, or soreness at the injection site
for a day or two are common and generally not a cause for concern.

Missing or delaying a dose isn't usually a crisis — most schedules can be
resumed and caught up on ("catch-up" schedules), and a pediatrician can advise
on timing if a dose was missed.

This is general information; the exact schedule and vaccine names vary by
country, and a pediatrician or local health authority should be the source for
an individual child's up-to-date schedule.
